OCEANS
In this unit, students will learn about oceans and some of the animals that live in them. Then they’ll research an ocean 
animal and make a model of it to add to a class diagram.  
Essential Questions
	
●What are the five oceans? Why has so little of them been explored?
	
●What’s a coral reef? Why is it important to protect coral reefs?
	
●How do you feel about sharks? What do shark species have in common? 
	
●How do a sea turtle’s parts help it to live on land and in water?

BEFORE READING
Remind students that in the last unit, they learned about the world and different places that are part of it. 
If you still have a paper plate map, take it out. Point out the different land masses that the class learned 
about. If you don’t have it, you can refer to any map. Ask: What large areas on this map have we not 
learned about? Explain that today, and over the next few lessons, students will learn about the oceans 
and the animals in them. Challenge their knowledge with this month’s Kahoot! quiz at ti.me/OceansKahoot 
before reading Deep Blue Sea as a class. 
2
DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
	
●Name the five oceans. (p. 2)
	
●How can people explore the ocean? (pp. 2–3)
	
●In which zone do most ocean animals live? (p. 4)
	
●Why do you think most of the oceans have been unexplored? (pp. 2–4)

DEEP BLUE SEA
CLOSING ACTIVITIES 
Return to the map you looked at in the beginning of the lesson. Call on volunteers to name each ocean. 
Which one is closest to where they live? Which ocean(s) have students been to?  
Return to the last of the discussion questions. Explain that some parts of the oceans are extremely 
deep. Remind them that some parts (the midnight zone) are so deep that there’s no sunlight, and there are 
few animals living there. Pass out copies of “Layers of the Ocean,” on page 3 of this guide. Make the bottle 
as a class. Discuss what students see in the bottle before giving them time to color the image of the bottle 
on the worksheet and label its zones. When they’re done, name and describe a marine animal. Have 
students point to the zone in which they think the animal lives. Ask questions such as “Can this animal be 
seen when snorkeling? When scuba diving?” They can use page 4 of the magazine to help.

BEFORE READING
Show students the cover of Explore a Reef and call on volunteers to describe what they see. Read the 
cover text aloud, and tell students that they’re looking at an image of a coral reef. Ask them to raise a hand 
if they’ve ever heard of a coral reef. Explain that a coral reef is an ocean habitat. Ask students what kinds 
of animals they think live in coral reefs. Invite them to share anything they know about coral reefs. Then 
tell them that Finding Nemo took place in the world’s largest coral reef system, and see if they have any 
other information to add. Read Explore a Reef as a class.
4
DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
	
●What’s a coral reef? (p. 2)
	
●What kinds of animals live together in reefs? (pp. 2–4)
	
●What’s notable about the Great Barrier Reef?? (p. 3)
	
●What does it mean to coexist? (p. 4)

EXPLORE A REEF
CLOSING ACTIVITIES 
To show students more of the Great Barrier Reef, you can use the Google Arts & Culture collection at 
ti.me/BarrierReef. Ask students to name some animals that live in coral reefs. Then pass out copies of 
“Coral Reef Count,” on page 5 of this guide, and crayons. Read the directions aloud, and give students time 
to count and color. Have them compare their sheet with a partner’s.  
Bring the class back together and return to the section “Reefs Are Important,” on page 3 of the 
magazine. Discuss what could happen if people don’t take care of oceans and reefs. As a class, 
research some ways to protect coral reefs. You can use the worksheet “Reef Rescuers” (2019) at ti.me/
ReefRescuers to have students make a pledge to protect coral. You can use these to make a coral reef 
bulletin board.

Sharks are at the top of the food chain.  
Meet these big fish inside.
Ocean Hunters
BEFORE READING
Start the lesson by asking how students feel about sharks. Call on volunteers to explain why they feel the 
way they do. Talk about sharks that students have seen in movies and on TV. What are these characters 
like? Show the cover of Ocean Hunters and read the cover text aloud. Explain that, although sharks might 
look scary and may be portrayed as dangerous, they usually stay away from people. Tell students they’ll be 
learning more about sharks today. Make a list of any questions they have about sharks, and see whether 
these are answered by the magazine. 
6
DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
	
●How many species of sharks are there? (p. 2)
	
●What do sharks eat? Are they carnivores, omnivores, or herbivores? (p. 2)
	
●Why do great white sharks leap out of the water? (p. 3)
	
●How do a blue shark’s teeth help it eat? (p. 4)
CONTENT STANDARDS
Science (NGSS): 
From Molecules to Organisms 
COMMON CORE STANDARDS (CCSS) 
RI.1, RI.2, RI.7, SL.1, SL.4, W.8
MATERIALS & RESOURCES
Class set of Ocean Hunters; class 
set of “Shark Facts” worksheet, on 
p. 7 of this guide; Internet access
OCEAN HUNTERS
CLOSING ACTIVITIES 
Were students’ questions answered in the text? If not, use this as a way to model how to research 
questions. Partner students up and tell them they’ll be researching a species of shark to share with the 
class. Pass out a copy of “Shark Facts,” on page 7 of this guide, to each pair. Read through all the things 
they’ll need to find out about their species. Assign a shark species, or let pairs choose one. The web page 
sharks.org/species is a good place to start. Give partners time to fill out their worksheet. Then they can 
present what they learned to the class. Discuss what different species have in common.
Close the lesson by asking students how they feel about sharks after learning more about them. To 
extend the activity, have them turn their shark species into a character in a book, movie, or TV show. They 
should describe what their shark is like, and the other sea animals it interacts with.

Turtle Time

BEFORE READING
Remind students that in the previous lesson, they learned about sharks. Tell them that today, they’ll learn 
about another ocean animal. Have them guess which one. Give them the following hints: The animal made 
a guest appearance in the issue about coral reefs. The animal can be found both on land and in water. 
Once someone correctly guesses it’s a sea turtle, show the class the cover of Turtle Time and read the 
cover text aloud. Ask students if they’ve ever seen a sea turtle, in real life, in a movie, or in a video. Invite 
students to share their experiences. 
8
DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
	
●Where are sea turtles born? (p. 2)
	
●What’s special about a sea turtle’s eyes? (p. 3)
	
●What do you think the trash on page 3 looks like? Why is it a problem? (p. 3)
	
●How does the Sea Turtle Care Center help sea turtles? (p. 4)
CONTENT STANDARDS
Science (NGSS): 
From Molecules to Organisms
COMMON CORE STANDARDS (CCSS) 
RI.1, RI.2, RI.7, SL.1, SL.4
MATERIALS & RESOURCES

TURTLE TIME 
CLOSING ACTIVITIES 
Remind students that diagrams label the parts of something. What parts of a sea turtle can they name? 
Tell them that these are turtle’s physical traits. Sea turtles have special parts that help them on land and 
in water. Pass out copies of “Turtle Traits,” on page 9 of this guide, to learn more. Talk through the different 
parts of a sea turtle before having students fill in the blanks independently. Then challenge them to the 
Try It! section of the worksheet. Have them use the magazine for reference. Discuss how the turtle’s parts 
help it survive on land and in water. 
To extend the lesson, invite students and their partners to make a diagram of the sharks they researched 
in the previous lesson. Print out pictures of their sharks and have them label fins, gills, teeth, and other 
distinguishing features. Put partner groups together to compare and contrast sharks.

Animals and Ocean Zones
ELA STANDARD (CCSS)
Research and Writing Projects
LESSON OVERVIEW
Students will choose an ocean animal to research 
and make a model of. They will add it to a 
diagram of ocean zones. 
MATERIALS
	
●Crafting materials such as clay, popsicle 
sticks, pipe cleaners, and pom poms
	
●Butcher paper or a large poster board
	
●Large index cards
	
●Images and/or books of ocean animals
Prep work
1.	Separate a large piece of butcher paper or poster board into 
four horizontal bands. Draw a sun in the top band. The other 
three will represent the zones of the ocean that students have 
learned about. 
2.	Choose ocean animals that students will pick from. Number 
an index card for each student. Prepare research materials, 
as needed, and decide what level of research is right for your 
students. At minimum, have a book or an image for each 
animal. Prepare research and a model of a sea turtle to use as 
an example.
3.	Decide what materials you want students to have access to for 
model making. Gather enough materials for each student.
Lesson flow
1.	Start by showing the class the paper that will be used to 
represent the ocean zones. Review what the zones are, and 
what differentiates them. Bring out the bottle that was made 
in an earlier lesson. Have the class work together to color each 
zone. Turn it into a diagram by using index cards to label and 
describe each layer.
2.	Tell students that they’ll be filling out this ocean poster with 
models of animals that live in different zones. Show students 
your sea turtle model, as an example. Then have students pick 
out of a hat, or choose an animal from a given list that they’ll 
make and learn more about. 
3.	Give students a picture of or a book about their animal. Ask 
them how much bigger or smaller than your sea turtle their 
animals might be. This will help them decide how large to make 
their model animal. Show students the materials from which 
they can create their animal. Provide ample time for students 
to build their sea creature. 
4.	When students are done crafting, bring the class back together 
to explain the research portion. At minimum, students should 
be able to identify the oceans and the ocean zone in which 
the animal lives. They should also be able to identify some 
interesting facts, like they did with their shark research. 
5.	Pass out numbered index cards. The number will correspond to 
the label for each animal on the poster. Ask students write the 
name of their animal on the top of the index card and to add 
facts to the lines. 
6.	Attach each animal model to the poster in the correct zone. 
Add a number to label each of them. Add the index cards 
around the poster for viewers to reference. 
Discussion 
Have the class take a look at the ocean diagram they created. Go 
through each zone, inviting students to tell their classmates 
about their animal. Then discuss which zone is home to most of 
the creatures, and why.
